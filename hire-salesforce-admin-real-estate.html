Hiring a Salesforce admin for a real estate investment company: the job post, the interview and the pay

You've decided you need someone to run Salesforce. Maybe your admin left, maybe your ops lead is drowning in requests, maybe you're about to add reps and a second market. This page is for the owner or ops lead doing the hiring. It has a job post you can copy, questions you can ask without knowing Salesforce yourself, a short paid test for finalists, what admins are paid in 2026 and what their first month should look like.

Short answer

Hire for your process as much as for Salesforce skill. Write the job post around how a lead moves through your business, ask scenario questions about missed calls, duplicates and cost per contract instead of certification trivia, and give your top two candidates a short paid test in a free Salesforce Developer Edition org. In 2026, US median base pay for Salesforce admins runs about $110,000 to $120,000 depending on the survey, with juniors around $78,000.

Do you need a full-time Salesforce admin?

Check this before you post anything. A full-time hire makes sense when there's a full week of Salesforce work every week: several markets, 30 or more users, a dialer and texting and accounting all connected, and a queue of requests that never empties. If you have a steady trickle of changes instead, a part-time or retainer admin costs less and covers more skills. I wrote up the trade-offs in hire, freelancer or retainer.

If your admin has just left, sort out access and the handover first. The two-week handover plan covers what to do before you hire anyone.

What are you hiring for?

In a home-buying or wholesaling company, the admin's job comes down to five things. The order matters, because it's also the order of what costs you money when it breaks:

  1. Every seller lead gets answered. New marketing numbers and lead sources mapped, leads routed to the right rep or queue, missed-call alerts and callback tasks working, duplicates caught.
  2. Follow-up happens without anyone remembering. Tasks, text cadences that stop when a seller replies, stale-lead reviews.
  3. Deals move from contract to close on time. Stages with required dates, tasks for the transaction coordinator, alerts for unsigned contracts.
  4. The owner's numbers are right. Cost per contract by campaign, the weekly report, dashboards that match the books.
  5. People and tools stay set up. New reps and VAs, permissions, people who leave turned off the same day, the dialer and other integrations working.

Notice that "build new things" isn't on the list. Good admins build plenty, but the job is keeping the machine running. Hire someone who sees it that way.

A job post you can copy

Most Salesforce admin job posts are a list of Salesforce features. Write yours around your business instead, and the right people will recognize themselves in it. Replace the parts in brackets.

Salesforce Administrator, real estate investment company ([full time], [remote or city])

About us. We buy houses directly from homeowners in [markets]. We're [number] people across acquisitions, dispositions, transaction coordination and marketing, and Salesforce runs everything from the first seller call to closing.

What you'll own

  • Lead intake: new marketing numbers, web forms and lead sources, duplicate rules, and routing to the right rep or queue.
  • Speed to lead: missed-call alerts, callback tasks, and text follow-up that stops when a seller replies.
  • Deals: offer, contract and closing stages, required dates, and tasks for our transaction team.
  • Reporting: the owner's weekly numbers, cost per contract by campaign, and fixing reports that drift.
  • Users: setting up new reps and VAs, permissions, and turning off people who leave on their last day.
  • Integrations: [dialer], [texting tool], DocuSign, QuickBooks, and moving what still runs in Zapier into Salesforce where it makes sense.
  • A written log of every change: what, why and who asked.

You have

  • Two or more years running a Salesforce org day to day, not only project work.
  • Flow Builder experience, including record-triggered and scheduled flows, and fixing failed runs.
  • Reports and dashboards, including custom report types.
  • The habit of testing in a sandbox before you touch production.
  • Clear writing. You can explain a trade-off to a non-technical owner in two sentences.

Nice to have: experience with real estate investors, wholesalers or another high-volume inbound sales team; [your dialer] or call-tracking integrations; the Salesforce Administrator or Platform App Builder certification.

Pay: $[range] base, plus [benefits]. Hours: [your hours and time zone].

To apply: send your resume and answer this in a few sentences: "A seller calls one of our marketing numbers and nobody answers. What should happen in Salesforce?"

That last question does a lot of the screening for you. People who've run a sales org answer it in terms of records, tasks and reports. People who haven't talk about the phone system.

Put a pay range in the post. Several US states and cities now require one, and it stops you interviewing people you can't afford.

Ten interview questions any owner can ask

You don't need to know Salesforce to run a good interview. Ask about your own business and listen for whether the answer is about records, rules and reports, or about vague promises. I've run Salesforce inside a home-buying company, so these are the things I'd want my own replacement to know.

Ask the candidateA strong answer sounds likeRed flag
1. A seller calls one of our marketing numbers and nobody answers. Walk me through what should happen in Salesforce.A lead is created or matched to an existing one, the source is captured from the number, someone gets a task or alert within minutes, and missed calls show on a report.Talks only about the phone system, never the record.
2. How would you stop the same seller from being created twice?Matching and duplicate rules on phone and address, a merge routine, and checks on what web forms and dialers create."We clean it up once a month."
3. I want cost per contract by campaign. What do you need to build that?A campaign or source on every lead, spend entered per campaign per month, contract status on the deal, then a report or dashboard that joins them.Jumps to a dashboard without asking where spend lives.
4. When would you use a Flow, and when would you call a developer?Flows for most automation (tasks, alerts, field updates, simple integrations). Code only for heavy logic or volume. Tests in a sandbox first.Wants code for everything, or has never used a sandbox.
5. We run a lot of Zapier between tools. What would you do with it?Lists every Zap, moves the ones touching Salesforce data into native Flows where it makes sense, and keeps a record of what still runs outside."Zapier is fine," with no inventory.
6. How do you make sure calls and texts from our dialer show up on the seller record?Checks the integration settings, field mapping and activity owner, then spot-checks real records every week.Assumes the integration "just works."
7. An acquisitions rep says Salesforce has too many clicks. What do you do?Sits with the rep, removes fields they don't need, and builds a simpler page or task queue for their role.Tells the rep to get used to it.
8. What would you check in your first week?Users and permissions, broken automations and error emails, duplicate counts, and which reports the owner actually reads.Starts building new things on day one.
9. How do you track a deal from contract to close so nothing slips?Stages with required dates (inspection, title, closing), automatic tasks per stage, and a report of deals missing a date.Relies on reps remembering.
10. What do you do with a request from me that would break something else?Explains the trade-off in plain words, offers an option, documents the change and tests it before release.Says yes to everything, or no to everything.

Ask four or five of these in a first interview. Save the rest for the finalists, along with the test below.

A paid test for your top two candidates

Ask each finalist to sign up for a free Salesforce Developer Edition org and spend two to three paid hours on this:

  1. Add a field to leads called "Last call result" with the values Answered, No answer and Wrong number.
  2. When it's set to No answer, create a callback task for the lead owner due in 15 minutes, and send the owner a notification.
  3. Build a report of open leads with no activity in the last 48 hours.
  4. Write half a page: what they built, what they'd test before going live, and what could go wrong.

Then have them walk you through it on a call. The write-up tells you more than the build. Anyone can follow a tutorial to make a flow. The candidates worth hiring bring up the edge cases on their own: what happens after hours, what if the lead has no owner, what if the rep is out sick, how to avoid creating three callback tasks for the same lead.

Pay for the test. It's a few hundred dollars, it's fair to the candidate, and it gets you real work instead of a performance.

Last step: have each finalist spend 20 minutes with your ops lead and one acquisitions rep. Those are the people they'll work with every day, and the rep will tell you quickly whether the candidate listens.

What do Salesforce certifications tell you?

The Administrator certification tells you someone studied how Salesforce works and passed an exam. That's worth something. It doesn't tell you whether they've kept a busy org running, handled a broken integration at 8 a.m. or said no to an owner. I hold the Administrator and Platform App Builder certifications myself, and I'd still rather hire someone who can walk me through a real org they've run than someone with more badges.

Treat certifications as a tie-breaker, and the scenario questions and the test as the real interview.

Where the person is based matters less than two things: how many of your working hours they overlap with, and whether they've seen a home-buying pipeline before. I work from Cairo during US business hours, so I'm not neutral on the first point. The second is the one I'd weigh more.

Red flags in candidates

  • Wants to rebuild everything in the first month.
  • Has never used a sandbox, or thinks testing in production is fine "for small changes".
  • Can't explain a past change without jargon.
  • No habit of writing down what they changed.
  • Answers every scenario with "we'd buy an app for that".
  • Talks about Salesforce features but never asks how your business works.

Questions

How much does a Salesforce admin cost in 2026?

US median base salaries for Salesforce admins run about $110,000 (Mason Frank) to $120,000 (Stott and May) in 2026, according to Salesforce Ben's August 2026 roundup. Salesforce Ben's own survey puts juniors at $78,000 and seniors at $109,000. Contract admins charge about $75 to $135 an hour.

Should our Salesforce admin know real estate?

It helps a lot. An admin who has seen a home-buying or wholesaling pipeline already knows what a missed call, a stale offer or a closing deadline costs, so you don't pay for the learning curve. Salesforce skills can be tested; process knowledge is harder to teach.

Is the Salesforce Administrator certification required?

No. It shows someone studied the platform and passed an exam. It doesn't show they've run a busy org. Use scenario questions and a short paid test to judge candidates, and treat certifications as a tie-breaker.

How do I test a Salesforce admin candidate if I don't know Salesforce?

Ask scenario questions about your business, like what should happen when a seller's call goes unanswered, and listen for answers about records, tasks and reports. Then give finalists a short paid task in a free Developer Edition org and have them walk you through it.

Can a VA be our Salesforce admin?

A VA can handle data clean-up, list views and simple report changes with the right permissions. Designing automations, integrations and security needs admin-level skills. If a VA does the day-to-day, have someone with those skills review changes.

What should a new Salesforce admin do in the first week?

Check users and admin access, failed automations and where error emails go, integrations and which user they log in as, duplicate counts, and which reports the owner actually reads. Then write a short list of what's broken or risky before building anything new.

Full-time admin, freelancer or retainer?

Full time if there's a full week of Salesforce work every week. A freelancer for one clear project. A monthly retainer for a steady stream of changes that isn't a full-time job. Many 10 to 50 person investor teams fit the third.
