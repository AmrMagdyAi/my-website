Which sellers should your team call first? Motivated seller lead scoring in Salesforce

When a team gets more leads than it can work, someone decides who gets called first. Usually it's whoever came in last, or whoever the rep likes the sound of. A lead score makes that call the same way every time. This page is for owners and acquisitions managers who want a score their reps trust, built from what actually leads to contracts in their market rather than from a template.

Short answer

Start with a simple points score on the lead, built from what your reps already ask: why they're selling, how soon, the condition of the property, whether it's vacant, and how the asking price compares with your numbers. Show it as A, B and C bands, not a raw number, and check it against your own closed deals every quarter. Einstein Lead Scoring needs at least 1,000 leads created and 120 converted in 200 days, costs extra on Enterprise Edition, and learns whatever "converted" means in your org, so most investor teams should start with rules.

Why score seller leads at all?

  • Order matters when you can't call everyone at once. Response time is one of the most studied levers in lead conversion: in Harvard Business Review's 2011 study, firms that reached a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it (sources here). The best leads should get that first hour.
  • It makes reps consistent. A new rep learns what your team thinks a good lead looks like on day one, instead of after fifty bad calls.
  • It decides who works what. Your strongest closer gets the A leads, and a VA or a cadence handles the C's.

What should a motivated seller score include?

The things your reps already ask on the first call, recorded as picklists rather than buried in notes:

  • Reason for selling: behind on payments, inherited or in probate, vacant, tired landlord, major repairs, relocating, or just curious.
  • Timeline: within 30 days, one to three months, three to six months, no timeline.
  • Condition: major repairs, some updates, move-in ready.
  • Occupancy: vacant, tenant, owner lives there.
  • Price: the seller's asking price against your maximum offer. See the deal analyzer.
  • Decision makers: whether everyone on title is on board.
  • Engagement: whether they called you, answered, or replied to a text.

Score the property and the situation, never the person. Leave out race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status and disability, the characteristics the Fair Housing Act protects, and anything that stands in for them, such as a seller's name. State and local laws can protect more, so check yours. It's the wrong signal and a real legal risk. Not legal advice.

A starting model you can adjust

The points below are a made-up starting point, not a formula from any company. Your own closed deals decide the real weights.

SignalAnswers and pointsMax
TimelineWithin 30 days: 25 · 1 to 3 months: 15 · 3 to 6 months: 5 · no timeline: 025
PriceAsking at or under your MAO: 25 · up to about 10% over: 10 · well over, or won't say: 025
ReasonBehind on payments, inherited, vacant, tired landlord or major repairs: 20 · relocating or downsizing: 10 · just curious: 020
ConditionMajor repairs: 15 · some updates: 8 · move-in ready: 015
OccupancyVacant: 10 · tenant: 5 · owner lives there: 010
Decision makersEveryone on title agrees: 5 · not yet known: 05

That adds up to 100. A reasonable first cut: 70 and up is an A, 45 to 69 a B, 20 to 44 a C, under 20 a D. Move the lines once you've seen a quarter of results.

Show the band, not the number. "A" tells a rep what to do. "62" starts an argument about why it isn't 64.

How do you build it in Salesforce?

  1. Add a picklist for each signal on the lead, and make them required when a rep marks the lead qualified, not when it's created.
  2. Add the points up. A formula field with a CASE() for each picklist is enough for most teams. A second formula turns the total into a band.
  3. Show why. A short text formula like "30 days · Vacant · Under MAO" next to the band. Reps trust a score they can read.
  4. Use it everywhere reps look. Sort queues and list views by band, and put it on the lead page and the mobile layout.
  5. Route on it. A leads go to your best closer with a task due now; B's the same day; C's into a follow-up cadence. More in lead routing for real estate teams.

One technical catch: a formula is calculated when someone reads it, so a change in a formula score doesn't start an automation by itself. If routing should react when a score changes, have a Flow write the score into a regular number field and trigger from that.

Keep a second, frozen copy: the score at first contact. Today's score changes as reps learn more. The first one is what you need to judge whether the model works.

How do you know the score works?

Once a quarter, run one report: leads created three to six months ago, old enough for deals to have closed, grouped by their score at first contact, with the share that reached contract and the share that closed.

  • The bands should separate clearly. A leads should reach contract at several times the rate of C leads. If the rates are close, the weights are wrong.
  • Read the misses. Look at closed deals that scored C or D, and at A leads that died. What did the score not see? Often it's a signal nobody records, like a tenant who stopped paying.
  • Change one thing at a time, and note the date you changed it, so you can tell what helped.

Check by source and market too. A score that works for PPC leads in one city can be wrong for cold-call leads in another, because the people and the reasons differ.

What about Einstein Lead Scoring?

Salesforce's own scoring learns from your past leads. The details, from Salesforce's help pages:

  • It needs at least 1,000 leads created in the last 200 days, and at least 120 of them converted. The same thresholds apply to each segment you set up.
  • Without enough data of your own, it uses a global model built from anonymous data across many Salesforce customers.
  • It reanalyzes your leads about every 10 days.
  • It comes with Sales Cloud Einstein, included in Performance and Unlimited Editions and an extra cost on Enterprise Edition.

The catch for investor teams is the word "converted". Einstein predicts lead conversion, and in many investor orgs a lead converts when an appointment is set, or never, because the team works everything on the lead or on a custom deal record. So it learns to predict appointments, not contracts. And it doesn't know your MAO math unless that's on the lead.

My view: start with rules everyone can read. If you have the volume and the license, run Einstein alongside them and compare both against closed deals each quarter. For where AI fits more broadly, see AI in Salesforce for real estate investors.

What usually goes wrong

  • Scoring what nobody asks. If reps don't ask about the timeline, the field is blank and every lead becomes a C.
  • Too many inputs. Fifteen picklists don't get filled in. Six or seven do.
  • Inflated answers. If whoever qualifies the lead is paid or praised on A leads, the answers drift upward. Score facts the rep records, and spot-check a few calls.
  • Never checking it. A score nobody tests against contracts is an opinion with a number on it.
  • Treating C's as dead. A low score means a different cadence, not no follow-up. Plenty of deals come from leads that weren't ready yet; see reactivating dead seller leads.

Questions

What is motivated seller lead scoring?

A way to rank seller leads by how likely they are to sell to you soon, using answers your reps record, such as reason for selling, timeline, condition, occupancy and asking price against your maximum offer. The score decides who gets called first and by whom.

How do you score leads in Salesforce without Einstein?

Add a picklist for each signal on the lead, add up points in a formula field, and turn the total into A, B, C and D bands. If automations should react to score changes, have a Flow store the score in a number field.

What makes a seller motivated?

Usually a reason and a timeline: behind on payments, an inherited or vacant house, a tired landlord, major repairs or a move, combined with wanting to sell soon at a price that works for you. Score the situation and the property, never the person.

How many leads do you need for Einstein Lead Scoring?

Salesforce says at least 1,000 leads created in the last 200 days, with at least 120 of them converted. Without that, Einstein uses a global model built from anonymous data across Salesforce customers.

Is Einstein Lead Scoring included in Salesforce?

It comes with Sales Cloud Einstein, which Salesforce includes in Performance and Unlimited Editions and sells as an extra on Enterprise Edition. Check your contract, because Salesforce renames its bundles often.

How often should you update a lead scoring model?

Review it every quarter against leads created three to six months earlier, using each lead's score at first contact. Change one weight at a time and note the date.

Should a low lead score mean we stop following up?

No. A low score means a slower cadence or a different person, not no follow-up. Many deals come from sellers who weren't ready the first time.
