The 6:40pm call

On-screen text and sound effects, no voice. A normal Tuesday. A seller calls at 6:40pm and nobody answers. Then what usually happens: at 6:41pm the voicemail lands in a shared inbox. The next day someone calls back at 10am and gets no answer. From day 2 to day 7 the status still says “new”. By then he called someone else. No one's fault: there's no system.

The fix is two rules. When a call is missed, a callback task is created and the seller gets a text. When a lead sits untouched for an hour, someone gets pinged. Nobody has to remember. How many did you miss last week? See what they cost you with the missed-call cost calculator.

The 5 leaks

On-screen text and sound effects, no voice. You don't have a lead problem. You have a leak problem, and there are 5 of them: 5 places money leaks out between the marketing dollar and the kept deal.

  1. Spend. The lead source report tracks leads by source, but contracts by source and cost per deal are question marks. Nobody knows. The fix: the lead source follows the deal all the way to closing.
  2. Answer. Two missed seller calls, at 6:42pm and 7:15pm. A callback task due in 5 minutes is created and assigned to the next rep. Replying within an hour makes you about 7 times likelier to qualify the lead (Harvard Business Review, 2011).
  3. Follow-up. Of five open leads, only two have a next step: a call on Tuesday and an offer on Friday. How many of yours have none? The fix: no open lead without a next task.
  4. Offer. Three reps offer $118k, $131k and $142k on the same house. On the example deal, ARV $250,000 × 70% is $175,000, minus $40,000 of repairs and a $10,000 fee, so the max offer is $125,000 and two of the three offers are over it. The fix: MAO lives on the deal as a formula, not in someone's head.
  5. Keep. Ten contracts signed this month and three fell out, so you kept 7, not 10, and ROI is overstated by 43%. The fix: ROI counts kept deals only.

Which one is yours? Score yours in 30 seconds, free, with the kept-deal map.

Working with me

We're about to hire a full-time Salesforce admin. Should we?

Maybe later. At a team of 10 to 50, most of the work is small changes every week: a new lead source, a new hire, an alert that broke. A retainer covers that for less than a salary, and the audit shows how much work there really is. Hire an admin or use a retainer compares the two.

We're adding reps or opening a new market. Should we fix the CRM first?

If you can, yes. More leads through the same leaks means more money lost the same way. The audit tells you what to fix before you scale, in the order I'd fix it.

We're on Podio, REsimpli or GoHighLevel. Can you audit that?

No. The audit is built for Salesforce. If you're outgrowing another CRM or spreadsheets, the right first step is a move to Salesforce: I map what you track today, move your data and build around how your team works. See how a move to Salesforce works, or plan it on a free call.

About Salesforce

Is Salesforce worth it for a wholesaling or home-buying team?

It depends on your size and how custom your process is. Investor CRMs are faster to start with. Salesforce makes sense when you need your own pipeline, reports leadership trusts, or more integrations than an investor CRM allows. I wrote the honest version, including when Salesforce is overkill: Salesforce vs investor CRMs.

How do you set up speed to lead in Salesforce?

A Flow runs the moment a lead is created: it assigns the lead to the right rep or queue, creates a callback task and sends an alert. Missed calls on marketing numbers create their own callback tasks, and a report shows how long sellers really wait. The full setup is in the speed to lead guide.

How long does a Salesforce implementation take?

Most focused builds take 6 to 12 weeks, scoped up front: the design first, then the core build (lead routing, pipeline, automations and integrations), then reports and training. Bigger builds take longer and are scoped before they start.
