Choosing a Salesforce consultant for an investor team, and what it should cost

This is for owners and ops leads about to hire someone to build, fix or move their Salesforce. I'm one of the people you might hire, so read it with that in mind. I've tried to write what I'd tell a friend who runs a home-buying company: how to tell the good ones from the expensive ones, what the market charges in 2026, and what you should never sign away.

Short answer

Pick someone who has worked inside a home-buying or wholesaling pipeline, start with a small fixed-price piece of work before any big build, and make sure you keep the org, admin access and the documentation. Compare quotes by what's included, not the hourly rate. In 2026, freelancers commonly charge $50 to $150 an hour and US partner firms $100 to $250, and small-business implementations often land between $10,000 and $30,000.

Two things the rate card hides. First, hours spent learning your business are hours you pay for, so a cheaper consultant who has never seen a wholesaling pipeline can cost more in the end. Second, the cheapest quote is often the one with the vaguest scope, and vague scope is where projects drag.

Salesforce licenses are a separate cost, paid to Salesforce. I don't quote list prices here because they change and they're negotiable. Teams can often get a discount through their Salesforce account executive, depending on the business and the number of users, so ask before you sign.

Eleven questions to ask any Salesforce consultant

  1. Have you worked inside a home-buying or wholesaling company's Salesforce? What did you build? Listen for specifics: missed-call handling, lead source by marketing number, offers and contract dates, buyer lists. "We've done lots of real estate" usually means brokerages, which is a different business.
  2. Who will actually do the work? Get names. If it's a firm, ask to meet the person building it.
  3. What would you look at first in our org? Good answers start with how leads come in and where the money goes, not with a list of features.
  4. How do you test changes before they reach my team? You want to hear "sandbox" and "we test with real scenarios before release".
  5. What's the smallest paid piece of work we can start with? A consultant who only takes big projects is a risk for a team your size.
  6. How do you handle changes to the scope? Changes are normal. Surprise invoices aren't. Get the process in writing.
  7. Who owns what you build? The answer should be "you do", including documentation.
  8. What happens if something you built breaks after go-live? Ask how long they support their own work and what it costs.
  9. How will you train my team? Acquisitions reps, transaction coordinators and the owner use Salesforce differently. One generic training session isn't training.
  10. When would you tell us we don't need you? If they can't name a case, they'll sell you something either way.
  11. What hours do you overlap with us? Seller leads come in during your business day. So do the problems.

Red flags

  • Quotes a full rebuild before looking at your org.
  • Can't explain what they'll do without jargon.
  • No written scope, or a scope that lists features instead of outcomes for your team.
  • Wants to be the only person with admin access.
  • Pushes a long contract before you've seen any work.
  • Answers every problem with an app you have to buy.
  • Talks about what Salesforce can do but never asks how a lead moves through your business.

Why you should start small

The best test of a consultant is a small, fixed-price piece of work with a clear deliverable. You find out how they think, how they communicate, whether they finish things and whether your team likes working with them, for a fraction of the cost of finding out halfway through a build.

A good first project ends with something you can use even if you never hire them again:

  • A written list of what's broken or leaking, with what each one is likely costing you.
  • A plan in order of impact, so you know what to fix first.
  • A couple of quick fixes done, so you can judge the quality of their work.

That's how I built my own audit, but the advice holds whoever you hire.

What you should always own

Whoever you hire, keep these in your company's hands from day one:

  • The Salesforce contract, in your company's name
  • System Administrator access for at least two people on your side
  • Integrations running under a dedicated integration user, not the consultant's login
  • Documentation of every automation and change, delivered as you go, not at the end
  • Everything built in your org in a form your next admin can open and edit
  • Passwords for your dialer, texting and other tools, held by you

If a consultant leaves and you can't run your org without them, you didn't hire a consultant, you hired a dependency. My admin handover plan shows what it takes to untangle that after the fact.

Questions

How much does a Salesforce consultant cost in 2026?

Freelance Salesforce consultants commonly charge $50 to $150 an hour, US partner firms $100 to $250, and offshore teams $40 to $80, based on ranges Codleo published in October 2026. Small-business implementations often cost $10,000 to $30,000, and managed support runs from about $2,500 a month.

Should I hire a Salesforce partner firm or an independent consultant?

For a 10 to 50 person investor team, an independent consultant who knows real estate investing is often the better fit: one person who learns your business and does the work. Partner firms make more sense for large, multi-department builds that need a team.

How much does a Salesforce implementation cost for a small real estate investment company?

Small-business Salesforce implementations commonly land between $10,000 and $30,000, plus Salesforce licenses. The range depends on how many integrations you need (dialer, texting, accounting), how much data you're moving and how much automation you want on day one.

How long does a Salesforce implementation take?

Small, well-scoped builds take weeks. A move from Podio or another CRM usually runs in phases over one to three months, with both systems running side by side for a short time so no leads are lost. A written scope is what keeps it on schedule.

What should be in a Salesforce consulting scope of work?

The outcomes for your team (for example, every missed call gets a callback task), what's included and excluded, who does the work, how changes to scope are priced, testing and training, support after go-live, and who owns the work and documentation.

Do we need a consultant if we're on an investor CRM built on Salesforce?

Sometimes. The vendor supports their package. Custom automations, reports, integrations and clean-up beyond the package usually fall to you, and that's where a consultant or admin comes in. Ask the vendor in writing what they support.

Can a consultant work alongside our existing admin?

Yes, and it's often the best setup. The admin keeps day-to-day changes moving and the consultant handles a build, a migration or a second opinion. Agree on who approves changes so you don't get two people editing the same automation.
