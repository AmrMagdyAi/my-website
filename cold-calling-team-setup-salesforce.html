Cold calling team setup in Salesforce: lists, outcomes and a scorecard that pays for appointments

The usual cold-calling complaint sounds like this: "my caller makes thousands of calls a week and I get no real leads." Sometimes the caller is the problem. More often the setup is: two callers dial the same owner, nobody knows which list is worn out, "interested" means five different things, calls are logged in a dialer that doesn't talk to the CRM, and the only number anyone watches is dials. This guide is the system around the calls: who owns which list, when to call, what each outcome does next, where the handoff happens, and the scorecard I'd use. It is written for owners and ops leads running one to ten callers, in-house or VAs, on Salesforce or any CRM you can shape.

Short answer

Give every list one owner and a fixed number of attempts. Call inside legal hours (8 a.m. to 9 p.m. in the person's local time) and scrub against the Do Not Call registry at least every 31 days. Replace vague call notes with a short outcome picklist where every value triggers the next step on its own. Hand off to acquisitions only when required fields are filled in, and score callers on appointments set, accepted handoffs and appointments kept, not on dials.

What are the six parts of a cold-calling system?

  1. List: where the names came from and how clean they are (importing a list).
  2. Queue: who calls which records, in what order, how many times.
  3. Call: the dialer and the script, logged back to the seller's record (dialers and Salesforce).
  4. Outcome: one value that says what happened and decides what happens next.
  5. Handoff: a real appointment or a warm seller passed to acquisitions with the facts attached (handoff checklist).
  6. Scorecard: the few numbers that tell you whether the machine works.

The calls themselves are part 3 of 6. If results are poor, there are five other places to look before blaming the caller.

Who should own a list, and how long?

  • One owner per record. Set the record's owner when the list is loaded, to a caller or a queue with a claim rule. Two people calling one owner is the fastest way to annoy a seller and start an argument about credit.
  • A fixed attempt plan per list. For example, a set number of attempts across a number of weeks, at different times of day, then retire the record to nurture with a date. Choose it from your own history: look at which attempt number produced your appointments and where the curve flattens. The cadence builder gives a starting plan.
  • Record the attempt number on every call, so you can see contact rate by attempt, by list and by time of day.
  • Retire lists on purpose. When a list's contact rate has dropped for several weeks, stop and ask whether the list, the numbers or the hours are the cause, before you buy a bigger list.
  • Keep one list per campaign. Mixing a pre-foreclosure list with a probate list in one queue hides which one works.

Which calling rules belong in the system?

Put the rules where a tired caller can't skip them. Two federal ones to build around (this is information, not legal advice; check state rules as well):

  • Calling hours. The FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule treats outbound telemarketing calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. at the called person's local time as a violation unless they've agreed otherwise. Store a time zone on each record (from the property's state and zip) and have the queue show a record only inside its window.
  • Do Not Call. Sellers and telemarketers must update their call lists against the National Do Not Call Registry at least every 31 days. Keep a scrub date on each record, and a company do-not-call flag for people who ask you to stop. That flag must beat everything else and copy to duplicate records.

Add a hard stop: a record with the flag set, or with a scrub date older than your limit, doesn't appear in a dial queue at all.

What should the call outcome picklist look like?

Notes are for stories. Outcomes are for the system. Keep the list short, fixed and mutually exclusive, and let each value start the next step automatically.

OutcomeWhat the system does next
No answerIncrease the attempt count; schedule the next attempt per the plan
Left voicemailSame as above, and flag that a voicemail was left
Wrong numberRemove that number; try the next phone on the record
Not the owner, owner reachableTask to get the right contact; note who was spoken to
Spoke, not interestedAsk for the reason (picklist); move to nurture with a longer re-contact date
Spoke, not nowRequire a re-contact date and a reason; create the task
Spoke, interestedRequire the handoff fields; assign to acquisitions with a callback due time
Appointment setCreate the appointment, confirm text if consent exists, notify the acquisitions rep
Do not callSet the company do-not-call flag everywhere; stop all sequences

"Interested" is the value that needs the most care. If it can be picked with nothing filled in, your acquisitions team will receive "interested" leads that no one can act on. That is the single most common reason a hot-looking list produces nothing.

When and how should a lead be handed to acquisitions?

Hand off when there is a seller with a real reason and a next step, not when someone says "maybe". The required fields at handoff are in the handoff checklist: reason for selling, timeline, condition, price expectation, who else decides and the agreed next step. Two system rules help:

  • A handoff creates a task for acquisitions due within a set number of minutes, and the caller sees whether it was accepted.
  • A handoff that sits unaccepted past its limit alerts the manager, and one that acquisitions rejects returns to the caller with a reason. This feedback loop is how callers learn what a good handoff looks like.

The stage rules around the handoff are in pipeline stages.

What should the daily scorecard show?

One line per caller per day, summed per week, from the CRM, with no spreadsheet:

MeasureWhy
DialsEffort and capacity, not performance
Contact rate (conversations / dials)Tells you about the list, the hours and the numbers as much as about the caller
Interested rate (interested / conversations)Script and qualification
Handoffs accepted by acquisitionsThe quality check: a handoff that is rejected didn't count
Appointments set, and keptThe number that leads to offers
Cost per appointmentCaller cost and list cost together

Pay and praise follow the measures you choose. If you reward dials you'll get dials; if you reward appointments kept, you'll get callers who qualify and confirm. It also gives you an answer when someone asks about a VA who makes thousands of calls a week with nothing to show: look at contact rate and handoffs accepted first.

How should you coach callers?

  • Sample recordings weekly. Listen to five calls per caller: two good, two weak, one random. Compare against the outcomes they logged. Mismatches (logged "not interested" when the seller asked for a callback) are the most common problem.
  • Review by outcome, not by caller. Pull ten "spoke, not interested" calls and listen for the pattern in the first thirty seconds.
  • Change one thing at a time. A new script and a new list in the same week tell you nothing.
  • Limit what callers can export. A caller needs their queue, not your database; see stopping reps exporting your list.

What if the numbers are bad?

Work down the chain, from the top, before the caller:

  1. Is the list the problem? Low contact rate on a new list usually points to stale numbers or the wrong hours.
  2. Is the data clean? Duplicates mean the same owner got called repeatedly and some stopped answering.
  3. Are outcomes honest? Compare logged outcomes against recordings.
  4. Is the handoff working? A high interested rate and a low acceptance rate means the qualifying standard differs between caller and acquisitions.
  5. Is acquisitions calling back fast enough? A great handoff that waits a day is a lost one; see speed to lead.
  6. Only then, the script and the caller.

Questions

What CRM workflow should a new cold caller start with?

A queue of assigned records, a short outcome picklist, a few required fields before a handoff, and a daily view of their own numbers. Add rules only when you see a repeated mistake.

How do I monitor my cold callers' metrics and calls?

Make sure the dialer logs every call to the seller's record with an outcome and attempt number, then report dials, contact rate, interested rate, handoffs accepted and appointments set and kept per caller per week. Sample recordings weekly to check logged outcomes.

What hours can I legally cold call sellers?

The FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule treats outbound telemarketing calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. at the called person's local time as a violation, unless they've agreed otherwise. State rules can be stricter. This is information, not legal advice.

How often do I need to scrub my call list against the Do Not Call registry?

The FTC says sellers and telemarketers must update their call lists, removing numbers on the National Do Not Call Registry, at least every 31 days. Keep the scrub date on each record and your own do-not-call flag as well.

Why does my VA make thousands of calls and get no leads?

Check in this order: list quality and contact rate, duplicates, whether logged outcomes match the recordings, whether acquisitions accepts the handoffs and calls back quickly, and only then the script. Dial counts alone say very little.

What should a cold caller be paid on?

Whatever you pay on, you'll get more of. Dials reward dialing. Appointments set and kept, and handoffs accepted by acquisitions, reward the work that leads to offers. Choose measures your CRM can report without a spreadsheet.

How many times should I call a cold list?

Set the number from your own data: look at which attempt produced your appointments and where the curve flattens, then retire the record to nurture with a re-contact date. A starting plan is in the cadence builder.
