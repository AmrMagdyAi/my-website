How many leads does it take to close a wholesale deal? Work it out from your own data

Everyone selling a course will tell you a number: so many leads for one deal. It is usually a number from their market, their marketing and their closing process, and it will be wrong for yours. The honest answer is that it depends on your lead source, your speed to first call, your offer quality and your follow-up, which is why the only useful number is the one from your own CRM. This guide shows how to get it: the seven steps in your funnel, the conversion rate of each by source, how to run the math backward from the deals you want, a worked example with made-up numbers, and the traps (small samples, slow deals, averaging sources) that make funnel math lie.

Short answer

Count the records that reach each stage (lead, conversation, appointment, offer, contract, closing) over the last 90 to 180 days, by lead source. Divide each stage by the one before it to get step rates, and multiply the rates to get leads per closed deal. Run it backward from your target to find the leads, calls and budget you need. Treat small counts as ranges, not exact numbers, and do it by source, because averages hide the channel that carries you.

Why do borrowed funnel numbers fail?

  • Different lead sources. Direct-mail responders, pay-per-click callers and cold-call connections behave very differently.
  • Different speed. A team that calls back in minutes converts more than one that calls the next day; see the sourced lead response statistics.
  • Different offers and markets. Your price discipline and your market's margins change the offer-to-contract step.
  • Different definitions. One company's "lead" is a form fill, another's is a qualified conversation. If the definitions differ, the ratios can't be compared.

What are the stages of the funnel?

  1. Leads: unique sellers (not records) who reached you.
  2. Conversations: sellers you actually spoke to.
  3. Appointments set: a call or visit booked.
  4. Appointments kept: it happened.
  5. Offers made.
  6. Contracts signed.
  7. Deals closed or assigned, with fee.

These line up with the stages in the pipeline stage guide. If your CRM can't tell you the count at each one, the system is the first thing to fix, not the math.

What is the method?

  1. Pick a window. Ninety to 180 days is usually enough. Shorter is noisy, longer includes old behavior.
  2. Count each stage by source, for sellers who entered the funnel in that window. For the recent leads, many deals won't have finished yet; either wait or only count stages that a lead has had time to reach.
  3. Calculate the step rate for each stage: stage count divided by the previous stage count.
  4. Multiply the rates from lead to closing. Leads per closed deal is one divided by that product.
  5. Add cost. Spend on the source divided by closed deals is cost per deal; the cost per deal calculator does it by source.
  6. Write it as a range. If you closed three deals from a source, "about one in 40" could easily be one in 25 or one in 70. Say so.

From 400 leads to 5 closings: 80 leads per deal. If that source cost a set amount over the period, divide by 5 for cost per deal. The same sheet for a second source might show 150 leads per deal but twice the average fee, which changes which one you scale. This is why you do it by source.

How do you run it backward from a target?

Say the goal is 8 closings a month. Working back with the invented rates above: 8 closings need about 11 contracts, which need about 55 offers, about 63 appointments set, about 250 conversations, and about 500 leads. Now you can ask practical questions:

  • Do I have the lead flow for 500 leads a month, and from which sources?
  • How many callers does 250 conversations need? The capacity calculator helps with the acquisitions side.
  • Can acquisitions handle 63 appointments, and at what point will speed suffer?
  • What marketing budget does 500 leads imply at my cost per lead?

That conversation is more useful than a vague "I want more deals".

What traps make funnel math lie?

  • Small samples. A rate from five contracts is a guess. Combine periods or report ranges.
  • Deal lag. A lead from last week can't have closed yet. Measure cohorts that have had time to finish, or count each stage separately.
  • Duplicates. Counting records instead of sellers makes your lead count too high and your rates too low; see duplicate seller leads.
  • Changing definitions. If "appointment" meant a phone call in March and a visit in June, the trend is meaningless.
  • Averaging sources. The average hides that one source is great and another loses money.
  • Counting activity as progress. Dials and texts are inputs. Judge the funnel on stages reached.
  • Seasonality. Motivated-seller volume moves through the year; compare like with like.

What does the CRM have to track?

  • Lead source and campaign on every lead from the first minute.
  • A date for each stage reached (stage history on the opportunity gives you this).
  • One record per seller, so counts are people, not rows.
  • Appointment kept or missed as its own fact.
  • Offer amount and date, and the reason a contract didn't follow.
  • Marketing spend on the campaign.

Where should you improve first?

Look at the step with the biggest drop that you can influence cheaply. In practice, that's often one of these:

  1. Lead to conversation: speed to first call, call windows, list quality, missed calls.
  2. Appointment set to kept: confirmations and reminders.
  3. Offer to contract: follow-up discipline and offer quality; see offer follow-up.
  4. Contract to closing: dispo speed and title coordination.

A modest improvement at an early, cheap step lifts everything after it. Fix one step, run it for a month, and rerun the math. If you'd like a second pair of eyes on your funnel, the Deal Leak Score is a quick first estimate.

Questions

How many leads does it take to close a wholesale deal?

It depends on your source, speed, offers and follow-up, so use your own CRM numbers. Count leads, conversations, appointments, offers, contracts and closings by source over 90 to 180 days, calculate each step rate, and multiply them. Treat small samples as ranges.

What is a good conversion rate for wholesaling?

There's no universal figure, because definitions, lead sources and markets differ. Compare your own rate at each step over time and by source, and improve the step with the biggest drop that costs least to fix.

How do I work out how many leads I need to hit my deal goal?

Run the funnel backward: divide your target closings by each step rate in turn to find the contracts, offers, appointments, conversations and leads needed, then check capacity and budget against those numbers.

Why is my cost per lead low but my cost per deal high?

Cheap leads often convert poorly further down the funnel, because of slower contact, worse fit or more shoppers. Measure each source to contracts and closings, not leads, and compare cost per deal.

How long should I measure my funnel before trusting it?

Ninety to 180 days is a common window, long enough for most deals to finish. For leads from the last few weeks, count only the stages they've had time to reach, and report ranges when you have few contracts.

What CRM fields do I need to calculate funnel conversion rates?

Lead source and campaign, a date for each stage reached, one record per seller, appointment kept or missed, offer amount and date, a dead or lost reason, and spend on the campaign.
