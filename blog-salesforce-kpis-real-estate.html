Real estate investor KPIs: what each role should track, and how to build it in Salesforce

Not everything you can measure in Salesforce is worth measuring. Teams that track everything end up with dashboards nobody opens and reports that don't change a decision. This guide is for owners and ops leads at home-buying and wholesaling companies who want a short list instead: the numbers each role should be judged on, how each one is defined, and how to build it so the numbers can be trusted.

Short answer

Give each role one or two numbers it can move. Marketing: cost per appointment and cost per contract by campaign. Lead manager: time to first contact and qualified leads passed. Acquisitions: appointments, offers and contracts, plus open leads with no next step. Dispositions: days to assign and actual against projected fee. Transaction coordinator: on-time closings and cancellations with reasons. The owner watches profit on deals that actually closed, by campaign and market. Write each definition down, build it from fields the system fills in, and check the money against your books every month.

What makes a KPI worth tracking?

  • Someone can move it. Every number belongs to one role. If nobody on the team can change it this week, it's a fact, not a KPI.
  • It's defined in writing. What counts, which date it counts by, which records are in and out. Two people pulling the same number should get the same answer.
  • The system fills in the data. Sources come from tracking numbers and forms, dates are stamped by automation. A KPI that depends on reps remembering to update a field is a guess.
  • It leads to a decision. If nobody would do anything differently when it moves, drop it.
  • It counts what you keep. Closed deals, not signed contracts. Contracts that fall apart shouldn't make a campaign look good.

Cancellation reasons by source are worth a look every quarter. A campaign that produces contracts which keep falling apart is more expensive than its cost per contract says.

Owner and finance KPIs

  • Profit on closed deals, by campaign and by market.
  • Booked revenue against forecast: the profit expected when each deal went under contract, next to the profit actually kept at closing. A gap that grows means underwriting or renegotiation needs attention.
  • Deals closed per month, by market.
  • Profit per acquisitions rep, over a quarter rather than a week.

For the Monday meeting, the short version is the Monday 5: cost per appointment by campaign, contracts per campaign, contract to first seller call, booked vs. kept profit, and campaign ROI that includes the houses you keep.

How do you tie KPIs to your accounting?

Salesforce isn't accounting software, and it shouldn't try to be. The CRM should know what each deal was expected to make and what it actually made. The books hold the money. Two connections keep the KPIs honest:

  1. Spend by campaign, every month, entered from the books or synced from them.
  2. The final fee or profit on every closed deal, matching what accounting recorded.

Some teams type both in once a month. Others connect QuickBooks Online through a connector, which is covered in QuickBooks and Salesforce for investors. Either way, reconcile once a month, because ROI built on spend that doesn't match the books is wrong however good the rest is. Campaign ROI by market and source, checked against the books, is one of the things I built for a two-market home-buying company in Ohio.

How do you build KPIs people trust?

  • Stamp the dates. A record-triggered Flow writes the date and time of first contact, appointment, offer, contract and closing as each happens.
  • Use picklists for source, stage, call outcome, dead reason and cancellation reason. Free text can't be counted.
  • Lock the original source once it's set, and copy it to the deal.
  • Require fields by stage, so a deal can't reach "under contract" without a closing date or "closed" without the final fee.
  • Save the history. Reporting snapshots store a report's results on a schedule, so you can chart trends week by week.

Questions

What KPIs should a real estate investor track?

By role: cost per appointment and per contract by campaign for marketing, time to first contact for the lead manager, appointments, offers and contracts for acquisitions, days to assign for dispositions, on-time closings and cancellations for the transaction coordinator, and profit on closed deals by campaign and market for the owner.

What KPIs should a wholesaling acquisitions team track?

Appointments set and completed, offers made, offer to contract rate and contracts signed, by rep. Also track open leads with no next step and overdue follow-ups, because those are the leads that quietly die.

What KPIs does a dispositions manager track?

Days from contract to an assigned buyer, projected against actual fee, deals with no buyer after a set number of days, and repeat buyers over the last 12 months.

How do I measure speed to lead in Salesforce?

Stamp the time of the first logged call or text on the lead with a Flow, and use a formula for the minutes since the lead arrived. Flag leads that arrive after hours and measure them from the next business morning, so they don't distort the number.

Should I track cost per lead or cost per contract?

Both, but judge channels on cost per appointment and cost per contract, and settle budgets on ROI from deals that actually closed. A cheap lead that never answers costs more than an expensive one that signs.

Can Salesforce track real estate KPIs with accounting data?

Yes. Enter or sync monthly spend per campaign and the final fee or profit on each closed deal from your accounting system, for example QuickBooks Online through a connector, and reconcile monthly so ROI matches the books.

How many KPIs should a real estate team track?

One or two per role, and about five for the owner's weekly meeting. Past that, people stop reading them.
