A Salesforce admin for your investor team: hire, freelancer or retainer?

Most home-buying and wholesaling teams of 10 to 50 people reach the same point. Salesforce runs the business, the person who set it up has moved on, and every change now waits on a support ticket or a favor. This page is for the owner or ops lead deciding what to do about it: what an admin actually does in an investor org, the three ways to cover it, and how to tell which one fits.

Signs the admin gap is costing you

You don't need a full-time admin because you're on Salesforce. You need some admin time when changes start piling up. These are the signs I see most:

  • Changes wait weeks. A new lead source, a new market, a new field for the acquisitions team. Nobody owns it, so it sits.
  • Automations fail and nobody knows. A missed-call alert stops firing after someone edits a flow, and you find out when a seller says nobody called back.
  • The dashboard and the bank account disagree. Cost per contract looks fine in Salesforce, but the money doesn't feel that way.
  • Your ops lead is the admin by accident. They know the most about the CRM, so every request lands on them on top of their real job.
  • Zapier is doing a lot of the heavy lifting. Every Zap is a thing that can break quietly, and the bill keeps growing.
  • You're about to grow. New reps, a new market, a new dialer. Every one of those is a set of CRM changes.

If two or three of those sound familiar, the gap is already costing you deals. The question is how to cover it.

Honest version: if you're under 10 people and nothing changes month to month, you probably don't need any of these. Fix the setup once and leave it. If you're past 40 or 50 people with several markets, a full-time admin starts to make sense, and a retainer can bridge until you find the right one.

What to put on a retainer

A retainer only works if both sides know what "covered" means. This is what I'd put in writing, whoever you hire:

  1. A request queue. One place where requests go, with a status. Not WhatsApp messages to one person.
  2. A monthly check of the automations. Flows, Zaps and integrations that should have fired, checked against what actually happened.
  3. A monthly report. What was asked, what was done, what's next, and anything that looks wrong in the data.
  4. Documentation as you go. Every change gets one line: what, why, who asked. The next admin will thank you.
  5. Response times you can plan around. Something broken that stops leads gets looked at the same day. A new report can wait for the next slot.
  6. Training for new hires. A short walkthrough for every new rep, so they don't end up working out of a spreadsheet.
  7. No long contract. If it isn't worth it, you should be able to stop.

If you hire someone full time

Good. A few things that save pain:

  • Ask them to walk you through how they'd track a lead from a mailer number to a closed assignment. If they can't picture it, they'll learn on your org.
  • Give them read-only access to a sandbox during the interview and ask what they'd fix first. You'll learn more in 20 minutes than from the CV.
  • Get the org documented before the handover, not after. If the person who built it is still around, pay them for a week of write-up.
  • Check that one person isn't the only system administrator. Two people with full access, always.

The 10-point health check also works as an interview exercise: hand it to a candidate and ask what they'd look at first.

Questions

Do I need a full-time Salesforce admin for a team of 10 to 50?

Usually not. Most investor teams that size have a steady stream of changes but not 40 hours a week of them. A part-time or retainer admin who knows real estate covers it, as long as automations are checked monthly and changes are documented.

What is a fractional Salesforce admin?

Someone who acts as your admin for part of the month on a retainer: handles requests, watches automations and integrations, keeps reports right and trains new users, without the cost of a full-time hire.

Can my ops manager just be the admin?

They can for small changes, and many do. The risk is that CRM work eats their real job and nobody checks the automations. Give them a backup, even a part-time one.
